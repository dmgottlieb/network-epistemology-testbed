The Zollman Effect

A community of scientists is testing a new treatment, B, that is in fact slightly better than the standard one, A. Each scientist only tries B if they currently think it is better, and everyone learns from the results of the colleagues they are connected to. Zollman (2007) found that communities which share less are more likely to end up right. Run the two networks below on identical evidence and watch how that happens.

The model

What each agent does, every round

This is the Bala–Goyal bandit model as used by Zollman (2007) and by Rosenstock, Bruner and O’Connor (2017). It is deliberately minimal: the only things that differ between the two panels above are who talks to whom.

  1. Hold a belief. Each agent has a credence c that B is the better action, meaning that B’s success rate is 0.5 + ε rather than 0.5 − ε. A’s success rate, 0.5, is known to everyone.
  2. Act on it. If c > 0.5 the agent tries B n times and records k successes. Otherwise it does A, which produces no new information, because A’s rate is already known.
  3. Share. Every result is seen by the agent who produced it and by that agent’s neighbours in the network, and by no one else.
  4. Update. Each agent applies Bayes’ rule to all the results it saw this round. Then the next round begins.

Because there are only two hypotheses, Bayes’ rule becomes simple addition in log-odds. One batch of k successes in n trials of B moves an agent’s belief by

logit(c′) = logit(c) + (2k − n) · ln( (0.5 + ε) / (0.5 − ε) )

So only the surplus of successes over failures matters, and with ε = 0.001 a run of 1,000 trials that comes out 497 to 503 moves everyone who sees it towards A.

When does a run end?

If every agent’s credence drops to 0.5 or below, nobody tests B again, no new evidence ever arrives, and the community is stuck on the worse action for good. That is the wrong consensus. If every agent rises above 0.99, the run is scored as a right consensus, since a reversal from there is very unlikely. A run that has done neither by round 10,000 is reported as unsettled.

Why sparse networks can win

Early on, a few unlucky batches of B results are likely somewhere in the community. In a complete network every agent sees all of them at once, so a single bad round can push everyone below 0.5 together, and then nobody is left to gather the evidence that would correct the mistake. In a cycle, the same bad luck reaches only a few agents. Others keep testing B, their good results spread slowly around the ring, and the doubters are eventually won back. The price is speed: sparse networks take far longer to settle.

Rosenstock, Bruner and O’Connor showed that this advantage is real but confined to hard problems: small ε, small n and small communities. Try the scenarios on the left, then measure it properly in the experiment below.

Parameters

NNumber of agents in the community.
εHow much better B is than A. B succeeds with probability 0.5 + ε; agents consider 0.5 − ε equally possible. Zollman used ε = 0.001.
nTrials each B-tester runs per round. Smaller batches are noisier, so misleading runs of bad luck are more common. Zollman used n = 1,000.
priorsStarting credences, drawn uniformly from the range you set. The standard choice is the whole interval from 0 to 1.

  • Hover any agent to follow the same person in both networks.
Monte Carlo experiment

How often does each network get it right?

A single run is a coin flip. To measure the effect, run thousands of independent communities, each with fresh priors drawn uniformly from 0 to 1, and count how many reach the right consensus. The simulations run in your browser.

Where this comes from

  • Bala, V. & Goyal, S. (1998). Learning from neighbours. Review of Economic Studies 65(3). The original bandit-on-a-network model that this page implements.
  • Zollman, K. J. S. (2007). The communication structure of epistemic communities. Philosophy of Science 74(5). Introduces the effect with cycle, wheel and complete networks of 3 to 10 agents, ε = 0.001, n = 1,000.
  • Zollman, K. J. S. (2010). The epistemic benefit of transient diversity. Erkenntnis 72. Same result with beta-distributed beliefs, and the idea that diversity of belief only needs to last a while.
  • Rosenstock, S., Bruner, J. & O’Connor, C. (2017). In epistemic networks, is less really more? Philosophy of Science 84(2). The robustness study behind the experiment presets. Reports, for example, 99.12% vs. 100% correct and 20 vs. 1,977 rounds for complete vs. cycle at N = 100.
  • O’Connor, C. & Weatherall, J. O. (2018). Scientific polarization. European Journal for Philosophy of Science 8. Same bandit, but agents discount evidence from people who disagree with them. A natural next model for this testbed.
  • Weisberg, J. (2020). The Zollman effect, How robust is the Zollman effect?, Mistrust & polarization. A clear blog walkthrough of these three papers, with code. The test suite here checks against its figures: .89 (complete) and .97 (cycle) at N = 10.
