Network epistemology testbed/Model 3 · Beta beliefs

Transient Diversity

Zollman’s 2010 version of the bandit drops a simplification from Model 1. Neither method’s success rate is known: each scientist holds a full probability distribution over each method’s rate. How firmly they hold it becomes a parameter, a measure of dogmatism. His result is that a community needs diversity that lasts a while, but not forever. A sparse network supplies it. So do stubborn priors. Too much of either and the community never agrees.

The model

Beliefs as distributions

Two methods, A and B, succeed with fixed but unknown probabilities: 0.499 and 0.5 in the paper. Each scientist holds a beta distribution over each method’s success rate. Every round they use the method with the higher expected rate, run it 1,000 times, and see their own and their neighbours’ results.

Learning by counting

A beta distribution with parameters α and β behaves like a record of α successes and β failures. Its mean is α / (α + β). Updating adds the new successes to α and the failures to β. Priors are drawn at random: in the paper α and β are uniform on [0, 4], about as open-minded as a prior can be. Raising that upper limit to thousands gives agents who behave as if they had already seen thousands of trials. They are dogmatic in the literal sense of being hard to move.

Why both A and B unknown matters

In Model 1, doing A taught you nothing. Here it teaches you about A, and that is enough to create the same trap. A community that has given up on B keeps refining its estimate of A to 0.499 and never collects the evidence that would show B is better. As before, the danger is everyone giving up on B at once. Sparse networks avoid it by letting beliefs drift apart for a while.

Dogmatism as a substitute

Stubborn priors buy the same time a different way: individuals resist a run of bad luck because they discount it against everything their prior says. With dogmatic agents the complete network does as well as the cycle or better, as the second scenario shows. Its agents still hear everything, so they eventually agree. On a cycle, dogmatists hear less, and if time runs out before the evidence overcomes their priors, the community never agrees. Zollman’s point: “transient diversity” needs to be transient.

How soon time runs out matters a lot. Judged at 10,000 rounds, as in the paper’s text, our dogmatic cycles still mostly learn; judged at 3,000, they fail as often as Zollman reports, or more. The experiment below has both presets.

Reading the panels

Compared with the earlier models

This is the model Kummerfeld & Zollman’s exploration (on What Is a Cycle Worth?) and Weatherall & O’Connor’s conformity model build on. Rosenstock, Bruner & O’Connor found its network effect, like Model 1’s, only appears for hard problems.

Monte Carlo experiment

How often does every scientist end up preferring B?

A run succeeds if, at the last round, every agent believes B is better. It fails either by settling on A or by never reaching agreement. Each point is hundreds of communities with fresh priors.
