Scientific Polarization

In the Zollman model there is one way to get stuck: everyone stops testing B, so no new evidence is produced. O’Connor and Weatherall (2018) add a second. Scientists trust evidence less the more its source disagrees with them. Now the believers keep testing B and sharing their results, the doubters hear every one of them, and the doubters still never move. The evidence is there; trust in it has run out. The model

Same bandit, uncertain evidence

Everything is as in the Zollman model except one step. Agents still hold a credence that B is better, test B only if that credence is above 0.5, and see their own and their neighbours’ results. But they no longer take a colleague’s report at face value.

How much to trust a result

When Jill hears that Ian saw k successes in n trials, she asks how far apart their beliefs are, d = |Jill’s credence − Ian’s credence|. She then decides how much to believe the report itself. In the version the paper calls “ignoring”, the result moves her only part of the way that a full Bayesian update would:

trust = 1 − min(1, d · m)    new credence = trust · (Bayes posterior) + (1 − trust) · (old credence)

That is Jeffrey’s rule written out for this case. The mistrust multiplier m sets how fast trust falls. With m = 0 everyone trusts everyone, and this is the Zollman model, run for run. With m = 2, a colleague whose credence differs from yours by 0.5 or more is ignored completely. Your own results always count in full, since you are at distance 0 from yourself.

In the second version, trust can go negative. A report from someone far enough away is taken as a reason to believe the opposite. Toggle “Held against them” on the left to see this.

A new absorbing state

Suppose the believers have all passed 0.99 and the doubters are all at 0.5 or below. If every doubter is at least 1/m away from every believer they hear from, nothing can change. The believers only grow more confident, and every result they share is ignored by the people who need it. The paper calls this stable polarization. In the belief chart it is the shaded band: with believers near 1, a doubter below 1 − 1/m is beyond their reach.

This trap differs from the Zollman one in a way that matters. In the Zollman model the community gets stuck because no evidence is produced, so more data per round helps only by keeping testers alive. Here the evidence is produced and delivered, and it fails anyway, so more of it does not rescue the doubters. What does help is evidence clear enough to convince people before beliefs drift too far apart. That is why polarization is rarer when B is much better (larger ε), as in the paper’s Fig. 3.

Things to try

  • Play the default. Watch the links between the two camps go dotted, then watch the believers’ results stall halfway across.
  • Set the right panel to m = 1. Polarization can’t happen, but consensus is slower. That is the paper’s Fig. 4.
  • Raise ε to 0.2. Is it easier or harder to find a polarized seed?
  • Put the mistrustful community on a cycle. It hears less, and it now polarizes more, the reverse of the Zollman effect.

Where this comes from

  • O’Connor, C. & Weatherall, J. O. (2018). Scientific polarization. European Journal for Philosophy of Science 8. The model: Jeffrey updating with trust that falls with belief distance (their Eqs. 1 and 2), complete networks of 2 to 20 agents, and the definition of stable polarization used here.
  • Weisberg, J. (2020). Mistrust & polarization, with code. An independent reimplementation. Its update order (one agent at a time, judging distance from credences as they stand) is the one that reproduces the paper’s Figure 2, so it is the default here.
  • Checks. With m = 0 this engine reproduces the Zollman model exactly, run for run (tests/polarization.test.js). With 10 agents, n = 50 and pB = .7 it gives 64% polarized at m = 1.5 and 92% at m = 2; the paper’s Fig. 2 shows about 64% and 91%.
