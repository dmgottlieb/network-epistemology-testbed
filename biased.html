Intransigently Biased Agents

From 1947 into the 1970s, US doctors prescribed DES to prevent miscarriage. Randomised trials had shown it did not work by 1954, and the drug was dangerous. Holman and Bruner (2015) model the doctors and a drug company that keeps reporting favourable results for its product, whatever the evidence. Unlike the propagandist in the Tobacco Strategy, this agent sits inside the scientific network and talks to every doctor. Both panels share the same priors and luck; only how the doctors are connected differs.

The model

A biased agent that never changes its mind

The doctors are Zollman’s 2010 agents (see Transient Diversity): beta beliefs about two drugs, both of unknown efficacy. Each round, each doctor gives the drug it thinks better to N patients and shares the results. The better drug works 51% of the time; the company’s works 50%.

The intransigent agent

The company only ever prescribes its own drug. Its reports are drawn as if that drug worked 50% + b of the time. The data are biased in ways that are hard to see from any single study, and it never updates on anyone else’s.

Why sustained truth is impossible in a static network

Suppose every doctor has settled on the better drug. Now the only information anyone gets about the company’s drug comes from the company, so every doctor’s estimate of it creeps up. Eventually one crosses over and starts prescribing it. That doctor’s honest results expose the bias, and the doctor and its neighbours swing back. Then the cycle repeats. The community can never rest on the truth, because only constant debunking keeps the company at bay. Holman and Bruner call this the need for “eternal vigilance”.

So, more links help, the reverse of the Zollman effect. Every doctor who tests the company’s drug debunks it for everyone it talks to. Sparse networks make each corner of the community repeat that work on its own.

Choosing whom to trust

In the dynamic version, each doctor keeps a bin of balls for every other doctor, itself and the company, and weighs each source’s results by its share of the balls. Each time a source reports, the doctor runs a one-sample t-test of the report against what it expects. A plausible result (|t| < 1.96) adds balls; an implausible one removes them:

bij ← bij + L · (1.96 − |tij|)

A strongly biased company gets caught: its results keep failing the test, its weight drains away, and the doctors settle on the better drug. A subtly biased one can stay trusted, especially by a doctor whose beliefs the company helped form. So, past a point, more bias is less effective, and bigger studies help because they make bias easier to detect.

Choices made here

  • Priors α, β uniform on [0, 4] for both drugs, as in Zollman (2010); the paper says only that beliefs are random.
  • The t-test uses the sample standard deviation of the N results; L = 1. Bins cannot go below zero, and start uniform on [0, 100].
  • In the dynamic network each doctor weighs everyone, its own results included, by trust. Static networks count every result in full.
  • The paper gives no N for its Fig. 2. With N = 5,000 we get its 42% (wheel) and 63% (complete).

Compared with nearby models

Unlike polarization, trust here tracks how plausible a source’s data are, not how much the source agrees with you. Like Learning Whom to Trust, the network is learned, but with a statistical test instead of being told the truth.
