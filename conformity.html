Conformity in Science

Semmelweis showed that hand-washing saved mothers’ lives, and his colleagues kept not washing. Weatherall and O’Connor (2021) model scientists who care about two things: the evidence, and doing what their colleagues did. With even a little conformity, a scientist can know the better action and still not take it. Then, because nobody tries the better action, nobody else learns it either. The panels share the same priors and the same luck; only the pull towards conformity differs.

The model

Payoffs for fitting in

The scientists are Zollman’s beta-belief agents (see Transient Diversity), facing arms whose success rates they don’t know. Each agent adds a payoff for matching its neighbours’ most recent actions to its expected payoff from each arm:

u(arm) = k × (neighbours who did that arm − neighbours who did something else) + expected success of the arm

Agents pick the arm with the highest u. With k = 0 this is the plain model. With k = 0.1, a single extra neighbour on the other side is worth 0.2 in success rate, four times the real difference between the arms here. Agents still update on all the evidence they see, so their beliefs stay honest. Only their actions conform.

Five ways a run can end

Without conformity there are two: everyone takes the better arm and believes in it, or everyone takes the worse one. Conformity adds three. Everyone takes the same action but some believe in the other (“with disagreement”), for better or worse. And polarization: two groups doing different things for good. Polarization needs cliques. In a cycle, an agent on the border between two camps has one neighbour on each side, the pressures cancel, and the evidence decides. In two tight cliques joined by one link, the bridging agent can know the truth and still never act on it.

Why conformity hurts

At high k the community is mostly playing a coordination game: it matters more that everyone matches than what they match on. The better arm wins little more often than chance. With four or five options, that chance is small.

Implementation notes

  • Agents choose one at a time, in a random order each round, so later choosers see earlier ones’ new actions. If everyone chooses at once, an evenly split complete network swaps sides forever, which the paper does not report.
  • An agent does not count itself among those it conforms to. Counting itself makes cycles polarize stably, which the paper says cannot happen.
  • Priors α, β are uniform on [0, 4] for every arm, as in the paper.

Things to try

  • Two cliques: watch the bridge agent turn blue while staying hollow.
  • Cycle vs. wheel at k = 0.1: the hub is the most pressured agent in the network.
  • Raise “Better arm pays” to 0.7: when the stakes are high, the evidence wins.

How often does the community end up doing the right thing?
