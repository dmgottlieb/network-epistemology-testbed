Learning Whom to Trust

Every model so far fixed the network in advance. Here nobody starts out connected. Each agent can look at the world itself (the diamond) or ask anyone else, and learns by reinforcement who has been worth asking. Barrett, Skyrms and Mohseni (2019) showed that networks self-assemble this way, with the unreliable deferring to the reliable. Pozzi (2025) adds vigilance: whether agents keep crediting a source that turned out wrong. Both panels have the same agents, with the same reliabilities, and the same luck.

The model

Urns that remember what worked

The agents face a long run of problems with true answers. Each problem, every agent starts with the false belief and has 100 rounds to get the true one, by looking or by asking.

Reinforcement

Each agent has an urn with one ball for nature, one for itself and one for each other agent. Each round it draws a ball. Nature gives the true belief with probability equal to the agent’s reliability. Asking another agent means adopting their current belief. Drawing your own ball means keeping what you have. Then the agent learns whether it ended up right. If so, it adds a ball with the same name, so whatever worked gets drawn more often. Urns carry over from problem to problem, so trust builds up.

This is the Roth–Erev style of learning Barrett, Skyrms and Mohseni use. Nobody reasons about anyone’s reliability; agents just repeat what worked. Even so, unreliable agents come to defer to reliable ones, and chains form where A asks B, who asks C, who looks at the world.

Vigilance

Pozzi asks what happens when agents are less discriminating about crediting their sources. Credulous agents always credit a source that was right, and credit one that was wrong with probability 1 − V. Sceptical agents never credit a wrong source, and credit a right one only with probability V. At V = 1 both are Barrett et al.’s rule.

The result is a network-size effect running in opposite directions. Credulous groups get worse as they grow: with 100 agents, everyone mostly asks everyone, and the few reliable observers are rarely asked. Sceptical groups get better: with more agents there are more reliable observers to find. Full vigilance does best in both, and best of all in big groups.

A link to the earlier models

Here the network is the outcome, not an input. The wish behind sparse networks in the Zollman model, that people not all listen to the same unlucky source, has a counterpart: vigilant agents concentrate their trust on the reliable, which is a kind of sparsity that tracks quality. In the polarization model trust also adapts, but to agreement rather than accuracy, and that is what breaks it.

Choices made here

  • A failed look at nature leaves the agent with the false belief.
  • Turnover happens once per problem: a newcomer gets a fresh reliability and urn, and others keep their old trust in that seat, unaware it has changed hands. Applying turnover every round instead makes all communities collapse, which Pozzi’s numbers rule out.
  • The truth is revealed after each choice, as in the original model, so reinforcement can depend on it.
