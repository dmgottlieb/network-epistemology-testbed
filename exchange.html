Network epistemology testbed/Analysis · Model 1 with exploration

What Is a Cycle Worth?

In the Zollman model a cycle beats a complete network because everyone-does-A is a trap, and sparse communication makes the whole community less likely to fall into it at once. Deliberate exploration attacks the same trap a different way. Here a complete network’s doubters sometimes test B anyway, and we ask how much exploration it takes to match a cycle. The answer is a single number per network, and it works as an exchange rate.

The exploration rule

Each round, every agent with credence ≤ 0.5 tests B anyway with probability x. We set x through a budget: the number of exploratory tests an agent would run if it doubted B for the whole horizon, so x = budget ÷ horizon.

Two mechanisms

Prevention versus cure

The violet ring marks an agent who tests B even though the evidence the community has gathered so far, pooled together with that agent’s own prior, says A is better. Both panels use the same mark, and it means something different in each.

In the complete network, everyone already sees the pooled evidence, so a ring can only be a deliberate explorer. With seed 4 the community gives up on B at round 15, exactly as on the Zollman page. Then nobody believes B for about 190 rounds while the occasional explorer runs a batch of trials. Good batches go to everyone at once, and eventually they pull the most optimistic agent back above 0.5, after which the whole community learns quickly. Exploration doesn’t stop the community falling into the trap. It gets it back out.

In the cycle, rings appear without anyone choosing to explore. They are agents who haven’t yet heard the bad news from the far side of the ring, so they act on a rosier picture than the pooled evidence supports. That is the cycle’s “exploration”: ignorance that happens to keep B alive. It is targeted in a way random exploration is not, since it occurs exactly when the community as a whole is souring on B. Its effect is to stop the whole community from ever being in the trap at the same moment.

The batch numbers show the difference cleanly (10 agents, ε = 0.001, n = 1,000). With any amount of exploration, the complete network still falls into the trap in about 10% of runs, the same as with none. Exploration changes how many of those runs it recovers from. The cycle falls in only 2.7% of the time, and with no exploration it never gets out.

The exchange rate

How much exploration is a network worth?

The curve is the complete network’s chance of failing to reach the right consensus as its exploration budget grows. Each horizontal line is a network with no exploration at all. Where a line meets the curve is that network’s exploration equivalent: the budget at which the complete network becomes exactly as reliable.

Chance of not reaching the right consensus

Complete network with exploration, by budget (both axes logarithmic). Three horizons, from 2,000 to 50,000 rounds, plotted together. Hover a row in the chart below to add its line.

Why budget is the right currency

The three curves lie on top of each other. Spreading 10 exploratory tests per agent thinly over 50,000 rounds or thickly over 2,000 buys the same reliability: 96.4% and 95.8%. What matters is how much exploring gets done, not how fast. That is what makes a horizon-free exchange rate possible.

The reason is that exploration only matters while the community is trapped. A trapped complete network acts like one agent waiting for enough good batches to climb back above 0.5. How many batches it gets depends on the total budget, not on how they are spread over time.

The cycle’s price

A cycle of 10 is worth about 14 exploratory tests per agent. That sounds like a lot, but few of them are ever run, because only doubters explore and most runs never have many. At that budget the complete network actually runs about 0.6 exploratory batches per agent, and it reaches consensus in about – rounds on average against the cycle’s 850.

Rosenstock, Bruner and O’Connor concluded that “there are better ways to improve exploratory success than to decrease connectivity.” In this model that is literally true. The cycle’s reliability costs a complete network less than one extra batch of trials per agent, and the complete network still settles far sooner.

What lines up

Very different shapes come out matched. The cycle and the line are worth the same, about 14. Cutting one link barely matters. The star, the grid and a random tree all sit together around 11 to 12, even though the star has one all-seeing hub and the grid has none. The wheel, the two-neighbour ring, the small world and the denser scale-free network cluster around 7 to 8.

The main driver is simply how many links there are. Fewer links mean less shared evidence, so beliefs stay less correlated, which is the correlation story from the Zollman page. Structure matters at the margin. Two dense cliques (21 links) are worth less than a ring with 20, because inside a clique everyone shares the same bad luck.

Caveats

What this does and doesn’t show

  • The exploration rule is ours. It follows the spirit of Kummerfeld and Zollman’s (2016) ε-greedy agents, who sometimes try the action they think is worse. Only doubters explore here, because exploring A teaches nothing when A’s rate is known. This page is an analysis built on the Zollman model, not a published result.
  • Exploration is nearly free in this model. An exploratory batch costs nothing except that, in a real setting, the explorer forgoes what it believes is the better action. Here B really is better, so exploring even pays. Real exploration has costs, and a fuller comparison would price them.
  • Reliability is scored as in the Zollman page: a run succeeds if everyone exceeds 0.99 within the horizon. The networks without exploration are scored the same way (their own runs end long before 10,000 rounds).
  • Random graphs are averaged over draws. The small world and scale-free entries draw a new graph each run. A sparse Erdős–Rényi graph is left out because at this size it is often disconnected, and a disconnected community can never reach a full consensus.
  • Sources. Zollman (2007); Rosenstock, Bruner & O’Connor (2017), In epistemic networks, is less really more?, whose §4 discusses Kummerfeld & Zollman’s exploration results. Precomputed with tools/precompute-exchange.js: 20,000 runs per network and per budget at the 10,000-round horizon.
