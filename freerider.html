Free-Riding on Exploration

Exploring pays the community and costs the explorer. When I try the risky idea and it flops, I lose the payoff; when it works, everyone I talk to learns from it. Kummerfeld and Zollman (2016) let each scientist choose their own exploration rate, ε, and ask what they will choose. The answer is a free-rider problem: each scientist explores less than would be best for everyone. Left to themselves, communities land in the low-exploration region where, as the exchange-rate page shows, sparse networks help.

Fifty rounds, one pull each. A safe arm pays 0; a risky one pays a draw with mean 1 and a large spread (sd 9). Each scientist pulls whichever arm has done better on average among the results it has seen, except that with probability ε it pulls the other one. The diamond is the hero, who can choose a different ε from everyone else.

One episode is mostly luck. This compares the hero’s average payoff with everyone else’s.

The game

What should one scientist do?

Fix everyone else’s exploration rate and find the hero’s best reply. Where the best-reply curve crosses the line x = y, nobody gains by deviating: that is the symmetric Nash equilibrium, what a community of self-interested scientists should settle into.

The hero’s best exploration rate

10,000 simulated communities per point, with common random numbers across the hero’s options. Best replies are read off a quadratic fitted near the best grid point.

Reading the curve

When nobody else explores (left edge), the hero should explore a lot: no one else will discover that the risky arm is good. As the others explore more, the hero’s best reply falls, steeply in bigger groups. With eight connected scientists, once the others explore at about 0.06, the hero does best not exploring at all and simply learning from them.

The curves cross x = y at about 0.24 for 2 scientists, 0.13 for 4, 0.08 for 6 and 0.05 for 8. The paper reports 0.24, 0.13, 0.085 and 0.06.

The dilemma

What scientists choose vs. what would be best

“Best for the community” is the ε that maximises everyone’s total payoff when all use it. “What scientists choose” is the Nash equilibrium. The gap is the free-rider problem, and it widens as the community grows.

Why this matters for the other models

Zollman’s original models assumed scientists never explore, and found sparse networks helped. Kummerfeld and Zollman show the helpfulness depends on how much scientists explore, and then show that self-interested scientists will explore little. The two results fit together: the “state of nature” of science is the low-exploration condition under which sparse communication substitutes for the exploration nobody wants to pay for.

Their recommendation is not to cut communication but to fix the incentive: “actively encouraging unpopular, risky science would improve scientific outcomes.”

The ring’s optimum is noisier than the complete network’s because its payoff curve is very flat near the top (see the exchange-rate page).
