The Tobacco Strategy

The tobacco industry rarely faked data. It funded real research and publicised the studies that went its way. Weatherall, O’Connor and Bruner (2020) model that strategy. A community of honest scientists, exactly as in Model 1, studies whether B is better. Policy makers (squares) never experiment; they update on what they hear. A propagandist (diamond) passes on only the results that favour A. Every result anyone sees is real. The science in both panels is identical, result for result; only what the public is shown differs.

The model

Misleading with true results

The scientists are Model 1’s Bayesian agents: B succeeds with probability 0.5 + ε, they test it while they think it is better, and they stop at a consensus. Three new roles sit outside their network.

Policy makers

They start sceptical about B, with credence uniform on (0, 0.5). They follow a fixed set of scientists and update by Bayes’ rule on those scientists’ results. They never act on the world themselves, so they cannot generate evidence that would correct them.

Selective sharing

Every round, the propagandist looks at all the scientists’ results and forwards to every policy maker the ones that came out in A’s favour, fewer than n/2 successes. Nothing is faked. But a policy maker who hears 5 scientists directly and 6 forwarded failures is updating on a sample in which B looks worse than it is. Each result is real; the collection is biased.

The outcome is a tug of war, and the paper derives its expected direction. Scientists’ results pull the public up and the forwarded results pull it down. More scientists heard directly, bigger studies (larger n) and a bigger true effect (larger ε) all favour the scientists. A long scientific debate favours the propagandist, who gets more rounds to work. So the Zollman effect cuts the other way for the public: a slow, sparse network that is good for the scientists’ own reliability gives the propagandist more time.

Funded research

The propagandist can also run honest studies of its own and publish only the ones that favour A. With a fixed budget it should run many small studies, because small studies give more spurious results. The paper’s policy lesson is the mirror image: a community that runs fewer, bigger studies gives the propagandist less material.

Journalists

Even without a propagandist, curation matters. A journalist who reports one result for each side every round, in the spirit of the old US “Fairness Doctrine”, never misleads anyone for good, but slows the public down relative to two random results or everything.

Choices made here

  • Each policy maker follows a random set of scientists, fixed for the run; the paper reports that the pattern did not matter.
  • Results with exactly n/2 successes favour neither side and are not forwarded.
  • Ten policy makers by default. They do not affect one another, so their number only changes how much the average wobbles.
  • Runs where the scientists themselves settle on A are set aside, as in the paper; the experiment reports how often that happens.

Things to try

  • Slide “Each hears from” up. How many scientists must the public follow to resist?
  • Raise n to 100: few results favour A, and the propagandist starves.
  • Funded small studies: change the study size and watch the forwarded count.

With selective sharing, our policy makers resist better when they follow most scientists: at 100% we get about 0.9 where Fig. 2 shows about 0.4.
